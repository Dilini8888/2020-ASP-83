1. Introduction

Heart disease is the leading cause of death worldwide, responsible for 17.9 million deaths annually (WHO, 2023). Early identification of high-risk patients using routine clinical measurements can assist clinicians in making faster diagnostic decisions. This project aims to predict the presence of coronary artery disease using machine learning models applied to clinical data from the Cleveland Heart Disease Dataset.

2. Data

The Cleveland Heart Disease Dataset was collected in 1988 at the Cleveland Clinic Foundation and published by Detrano et al. (1989) in the American Journal of Cardiology. It is publicly available from the UCI Machine Learning Repository. The dataset contains 303 patients and 14 clinical variables including age, sex, chest pain type, resting blood pressure, cholesterol, fasting blood sugar, resting ECG results, maximum heart rate, exercise induced angina, ST depression, slope, number of vessels, thalassemia type and the target variable indicating presence of heart disease.

7. Results and Discussion

The Random Forest model achieved the highest accuracy of 83.33% and AUC of 0.91, outperforming Logistic Regression (81.67%, AUC 0.87) and SVM (80.00%, AUC 0.86). Feature importance analysis revealed that maximum heart rate (thalach), number of major vessels (ca), and thalassemia type (thal) were the strongest predictors of heart disease. Statistical tests confirmed significant differences between disease and non-disease groups for age, max heart rate, blood pressure, sex, chest pain type and exercise angina (p < 0.05). Cholesterol was not statistically significant (p = 0.136), which is a surprising but documented finding in this dataset. The k-means clustering analysis identified two natural patient clusters aligned with age and heart rate, consistent with the classification results.

8. Conclusion

This project successfully demonstrated that machine learning models can predict coronary artery disease from routine clinical measurements with over 80% accuracy. The Random Forest model was the best performer with an AUC of 0.91. Maximum heart rate and number of major vessels were identified as the most important diagnostic features. Future work could include larger and more diverse datasets, additional feature engineering, and deep learning approaches to further improve prediction accuracy.
